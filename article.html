Legal Liability Arising from AI in Indonesia

>

The integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) across various sectors in Indonesia has triggered a legal vacuum (rechtshiaat), particularly within the realm of private law when these autonomous systems cause material or immaterial damages to third parties. This article aims to analyze the adequacy of Indonesia's positive civil law instruments in resolving liability disputes arising from AI technology. Utilizing a normative legal research method through a statutory approach and a conceptual approach, this study examines the limitations of the fault-based liability doctrine under Article 1365 of the Indonesian Civil Code (KUHPerdata) and product liability under Law No. 8 of 1999 on Consumer Protection. The research findings indicate that the autonomous and opaque (black box) nature of AI disrupts the traditional chain of causation, thereby complicating the victim's ability to prove the element of human fault. As a legal reconstruction, this article proposes the implementation of a strict liability doctrine and a compulsory insurance scheme for high-risk AI developers or operators to ensure distributive justice in the digital economy era.

The integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) into various sectors in Indonesia has created a legal vacuum (rechtshiaat), particularly in the realm of civil law when such autonomous systems cause material or immaterial losses to third parties. This article aims to analyze the advisability of Indonesia's positive civil law instruments in resolving disputes over losses resulting from AI technology. Using normative legal research methods through a statute approach and a conceptual approach, this study examines the limitations of the doctrine of fault-based liability in Article 1365 of the Civil Code and product liability in Law No. 8 of 1999 concerning Consumer Protection. The results show that the autonomous and opaque (black box) nature of AI breaks the traditional chain of causality, making it difficult for victims to prove human error. As a legal reconstruction, this article proposes the application of the doctrine of strict liability and compulsory insurance for high-risk AI developers or operators to ensure distributive justice in the digital economy era.

1. Introduction

The acceleration of digital technology in Indonesia has shifted from being a mere transactional tool to an independent decision-making system through the use of Artificial Intelligence (AI). The government's blueprint, the 2020-2045 National Artificial Intelligence Strategy, affirms Indonesia's commitment to adopting AI across key pillars, including food security, healthcare, bureaucratic efficiency, and the financial industry (fintech). However, this techno-economic enthusiasm has not been matched by adequate national legal structure and substance.

A fundamental problem arises when AI systems—driven by deep learning algorithms and neural networks—exhibit a high degree of autonomy in executing actions. AI is no longer a static instrument (like conventional computers), but rather a dynamic subject capable of "self-learning" from big data and producing outputs that cannot be predicted with certainty by either its developers or its users.

Indonesia's civil law system, primarily based on the colonial Civil Code (KUHPerdata), is built on an anthropocentric premise. This means that the law only recognizes two legal subjects, the subjects supporting rights and obligations: humans (natuurlijke persoon) and legal entities (rechtspersoon). AI, no matter how intelligent, is a legal object (zaak) that lacks legal personhood.

If a loss occurs, a conventional lawsuit based on Article 1365 of the Civil Code concerning Unlawful Acts (PMH) will encounter technical and doctrinal impasses. These obstacles include:

  • The Black Box Problem: The internal complexity of AI computing processes makes the algorithm's reasoning impossible for humans, including legal experts, to understand with the naked eye.
  • Absence of Conventional Error Elements: How to prove the existence of negligence (culpa) or intent (dolus) in humans if the loss arises purely due to an algorithmic drift that occurs autonomously beyond the programmer's control?

Several academics have attempted to implement Law No. 8 of 1999 concerning Consumer Protection (the Consumer Protection Law) through a product liability regime. However, the Consumer Protection Law still leaves gaps in interpretation regarding whether artificial intelligence software can be categorized as "goods" or "services," and how to prove "product defects" in systems that continue to evolve after they are marketed.

Therefore, this article comprehensively examines the limitations of positive civil law doctrine in Indonesia in responding to losses due to AI, and formulates a model for reconstructing legal liability that is oriented towards victim protection and legal certainty for technological innovation.

2. Research Methods

This research is normative legal research, which examines written law from an internal perspective with a focus on the coherence of legal dogmatics towards new technological phenomena.

2.1 Research Approach

  1. statutory Approach: Reviewing related regulations, including the Civil Code (especially Book III on Contracts), the Consumer Protection Law, and Law No. 1 of 2024 concerning the Second Amendment to the Electronic Information and Transactions Law (ITE Law).
  2. Conceptual Approach: Dissecting the concept of legal subjects, the doctrine of causality, the doctrine of fault, and the shift towards strict liability in domestic and international legal literature.

2.2 Data Sources and Analysis

Secondary data consisting of primary legal materials (regulations), secondary legal materials (scientific journals, books, court decisions related to electronic systems), and tertiary legal materials (legal dictionaries) were collected through literature review. Data analysis was conducted qualitatively using deductive reasoning—drawing specific conclusions from general legal premises to address the legal issues being studied.
